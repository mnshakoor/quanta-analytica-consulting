Executive Summary

The Political Unrest Flashpoint and Escalation Assessment Method (PU-FEAM) is an event-based, time-series framework for evaluating whether political and civil unrest is stable, intensifying, approaching a flashpoint, transitioning into sustained political violence, or moving toward systemic instability. It examines documented developments over a rolling 6- to 12-month period while assigning greater analytic weight to recent events, behavioral change, interacting escalation mechanisms, and foreseeable triggers.

PU-FEAM is designed to complement the Quanta Analytic Process (QAP). It does not replace Key Assumptions Checks, Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, indicators and warnings, scenario analysis, or CARVER. Its purpose is to convert disparate political, security, social, and informational events into a structured warning assessment with an explicit score, trajectory, confidence level, and decision-relevant explanation.

The model is built around a central proposition: political unrest is most likely to escalate when accumulated grievance, organized mobilization, declining restraint, a catalytic event, and weakened containment capacity converge. No single indicator is determinative. Analysts must assess interaction effects, direction of travel, and threshold-crossing behavior.

02

I. Analytic Purpose

PU-FEAM is intended to answer five principal questions:

  • Pressure: How much political and social pressure has accumulated?
  • Mobilization: Are political actors becoming increasingly capable and willing to mobilize?
  • Friction: Are interactions among protesters, opposition groups, government authorities, and security forces becoming more confrontational?
  • Trigger susceptibility: Could a discrete event rapidly convert existing tension into widespread unrest?
  • Containment: Do institutions retain the legitimacy, capacity, and willingness to manage the situation without excessive force or political breakdown?

The model does not simply count protests. Large numbers of peaceful demonstrations may indicate political participation rather than imminent escalation. The more important question is whether the character, frequency, geographic reach, organization, rhetoric, repression, and violence associated with political activity are changing. Event-data methodologies such as ACLED distinguish among peaceful protests, riots, violence against civilians, battles, and strategically important nonviolent developments, providing a useful foundation for this distinction [1][2].

03

II. Core Analytic Proposition

Political unrest is most likely to escalate when five conditions converge:

Accumulated grievance + organized mobilization + declining restraint + catalytic event + weakened containment capacity

Anger without organization may generate sporadic demonstrations. Organization without broad grievance may produce limited partisan activity. Repression without opposition capacity may generate temporary political silence. A trigger without accumulated pressure may produce only a localized reaction. By contrast, strong grievance, mobilization, repression, and an emotionally resonant trigger can produce rapid escalation.

04

III. Assessment Structure

Layer Function
Layer 1: Structural Pressure Conditions that make unrest more likely but do not establish that escalation is imminent.
Layer 2: Dynamic Escalation Indicators Observable changes in behavior during the 6- to 12-month assessment period.
Layer 3: Flashpoint Triggers Events capable of rapidly accelerating unrest.
Layer 4: Containment and Resilience Factors that can prevent, absorb, redirect, or de-escalate unrest.
05

IV. Indicator Architecture

PU-FEAM uses 10 indicator domains. Each domain is scored from 0 to 5 and weighted according to its relevance to escalation.

Score General Meaning
0 No meaningful evidence
1 Limited or isolated
2 Present but generally contained
3 Significant and increasing
4 Severe, widespread, or accelerating
5 Extreme, systemic, or immediately escalatory

Domain 1: Public Grievance and Legitimacy Crisis

Weight: 10%

  • Declining confidence in government
  • Perceptions of corruption, exclusion, or elite capture
  • Electoral legitimacy disputes
  • Economic grievances linked to political blame
  • Inflation, unemployment, subsidy removal, or austerity
  • Regional, ethnic, religious, or class-based marginalization
  • Public-service failures
  • Perceived impunity
  • Unresolved political scandals
  • A widening gap between official narratives and lived conditions

The key issue is whether hardship is increasingly attributed to political actors, understood as deliberate or unjust, shared across constituencies, incorporated into opposition narratives, and connected to demands for systemic change.

Domain 2: Protest Frequency, Scale, and Geographic Diffusion

Weight: 12%

  • Number, frequency, duration, and estimated size of protest events
  • Expansion from capitals to secondary cities
  • Simultaneous demonstrations in multiple regions
  • Recurring protest locations
  • Participation by new demographic or occupational groups
  • Transition from issue-specific to anti-government demands
  • Increased use of strikes, occupations, roadblocks, and sit-ins
  • Expansion from symbolic protest to disruption of critical functions

A high-risk pattern is more frequent protest, wider geographic distribution, broader coalition participation, and increasingly systemic demands. Raw event totals should be normalized against the country’s historical baseline.

Domain 3: Opposition Organization and Mobilization Capacity

Weight: 12%

  • Coalition formation among opposition parties
  • Coordination among political, labor, student, religious, and civil-society organizations
  • Emergence of identifiable protest leadership
  • Ability to issue and sustain national calls to action
  • Fundraising, transportation, legal defense, medical, and casualty-support networks
  • Diaspora financing or advocacy
  • Creation of local committees
  • Use of resilient communications
  • Capacity to sustain activity after arrests
  • Development of parallel political structures
  • Defections by former government supporters

The key transition is from spontaneous outrage to coordinated mobilization and then to sustained campaign infrastructure.

Domain 4: Political Discourse, Polarization, and Threat Narratives

Weight: 10%

  • Dehumanizing or exclusionary language
  • Treason, enemy, terrorist, or foreign-agent labels
  • Claims that political survival is existential
  • Public threats against opponents
  • Collective blame against communities
  • Delegitimization of elections, courts, or constitutional processes
  • Political endorsement of vigilantism
  • Calls for revenge or purification
  • Growing rumor and conspiracy narratives
  • Glorification of political violence
  • Declining willingness to recognize counterarguments

V-Dem provides disaggregated measures relevant to political polarization, civil liberties, civil-society repression, reasoned justification, and respect for counterarguments [3]. The most concerning combination is existential rhetoric, dehumanization, and claims that peaceful institutions are illegitimate.

Domain 5: State Suppression and Coercive Escalation

Weight: 14%

  • Arrests or preventive detention of opposition figures
  • Protest bans and emergency decrees
  • Internet shutdowns and media restrictions
  • Closure of civil-society organizations
  • Surveillance and intimidation
  • Use of tear gas, rubber projectiles, live ammunition, or lethal force
  • Collective punishment, disappearance, torture, or extrajudicial-killing allegations
  • Deployment of military units for public-order functions
  • Politicization of police or intelligence services
  • Designation of opposition groups as extremist or terrorist entities
  • Restrictions on assembly, speech, or association
  • Impunity for violence by state-aligned groups

OHCHR identifies widespread or systematic human-rights violations as potential indicators of increased conflict, violence, or instability [4]. Analysts must test whether repression is deterring participation, displacing activity, increasing anger, radicalizing actors, fragmenting the opposition, or unifying previously divided groups.

Domain 6: Protester and Opposition Tactical Escalation

Weight: 12%

  • Movement from marches to strikes, sit-ins, roadblocks, occupations, and infrastructure disruption
  • Use of masks, shields, helmets, or improvised protection
  • Construction of barricades
  • Use of incendiaries or destructive tactics
  • Targeting of government buildings or police facilities
  • Sabotage of transport or energy infrastructure
  • Formation of neighborhood defense groups
  • Retaliatory attacks
  • Movement from opportunistic violence to planned operations
  • Tactical learning across protest events
  • Cross-border transfer of methods, financing, or equipment

Analysts must distinguish violence by protesters from infiltration, criminal opportunism, state-aligned provocation, organized opposition violence, and communal violence occurring alongside political unrest.

Domain 7: Security-Force Cohesion and Behavior

Weight: 10%

  • Refusal to enforce orders
  • Uneven enforcement across regions
  • Police or military defections
  • Public criticism by senior officers
  • Replacement or purge of commanders
  • Increased use of irregular or party-aligned forces
  • Rivalries among police, military, intelligence, and paramilitary organizations
  • Delayed salary payments or barracks unrest
  • Ethnic, regional, or political divisions within security forces
  • Neutrality declarations or competing chains of command

Security-force fragmentation is often more consequential than the absolute size of demonstrations. A government may withstand large protests while coercive institutions remain cohesive, while smaller unrest can become destabilizing when security forces split.

Domain 8: Elite Fragmentation and Institutional Breakdown

Weight: 8%

  • Cabinet resignations or ruling-party defections
  • Legislative rebellion
  • Judicial confrontation with the executive
  • Public disputes among senior officials
  • Regional leaders rejecting national directives
  • Business elites withdrawing support
  • Former regime insiders joining opposition campaigns
  • Constitutional disputes and institutional paralysis
  • Competing claims to political authority
  • Delayed or contested succession
  • Efforts to alter election rules during crisis
  • Collapse of negotiations

Elite fragmentation can create openings for opposition mobilization while increasing uncertainty over political authority and command.

Domain 9: Information Environment and Sentiment Acceleration

Weight: 6%

  • Rapid increases in protest-related discussion
  • Growth in anger, fear, humiliation, desperation, revenge, or moral-outrage narratives
  • Viral images of casualties or abuse
  • Coordinated hashtags or calls to action
  • Rumors of atrocities or electoral fraud
  • Fabricated content intended to provoke retaliation
  • Foreign information operations
  • Government narrative-control campaigns
  • Internet restrictions and circumvention activity
  • Movement from complaint to mobilizing language
  • Martyrdom symbolism and celebration of violence

Sentiment should not be reduced to positive or negative. The most escalatory combination is often moral outrage, solidarity, and perceived efficacy. Anger combined with fatalism may not mobilize; anger combined with organization and perceived ability to produce change may.

Domain 10: Trigger Exposure and Flashpoint Proximity

Weight: 6%

  • Elections or contested results
  • Court rulings
  • Arrest or assassination of a political figure
  • Death of a protester in custody
  • Video of security-force abuse
  • Removal of subsidies or sudden price increases
  • Constitutional amendments or dissolution of parliament
  • Religious or ethnic incidents
  • Commemorative dates
  • Major corruption revelations
  • Forced eviction or land seizure
  • Labor action
  • Highly symbolic funerals
  • Failed negotiations
  • Coup attempt or foreign intervention

Each trigger should be evaluated for probability, timing, symbolic resonance, affected population, narrative exploitability, mobilization readiness, and government response options.

06

V. Baseline Weighted Score

Each domain is scored from 0 to 5. The weighted base score is calculated as follows:

Base Risk Score = Σ [(Domain Score ÷ 5) × Domain Weight]

The result ranges from 0 to 100.

Score Condition Interpretation
0-19 Stable Political pressure remains limited or institutionally manageable.
20-34 Guarded Grievances or mobilization are present but escalation remains constrained.
35-49 Elevated Several escalation mechanisms are active.
50-64 High Unrest is intensifying or becoming increasingly difficult to contain.
65-79 Severe A flashpoint or major escalation is plausible in the near term.
80-100 Critical Escalation is occurring, imminent, or approaching systemic instability.

The base score must be interpreted alongside trend, acceleration, trigger exposure, resilience, and confidence.

07

VI. Temporal Analysis

A. Rolling Observation Windows

Window Function
Previous 12 months Establish structural and behavioral baselines and account for election cycles, seasonal protest patterns, religious periods, annual commemorations, agricultural cycles, and academic calendars.
Previous 6 months Identify sustained directional change.
Previous 30-90 days Detect acceleration and flashpoint proximity.

B. Time Weighting

Event Recency Suggested Weight
0-30 days 1.00
31-90 days 0.85
91-180 days 0.65
181-365 days 0.40

Recency weighting prevents a year-old event from carrying the same warning value as a similar event occurring within the previous week.

08

VII. Trend and Acceleration Assessment

Code Meaning Adjustment
↓↓ Rapidly improving -2
↓ Improving -1
→ Stable or mixed 0
↑ Deteriorating +1
↑↑ Rapidly deteriorating +2

The combined Trend Adjustment should range from -10 to +10 points.

Acceleration Test

A situation is accelerating when at least three of the following are present:

  • Event frequency increases substantially
  • Event severity increases
  • Geographic spread expands
  • New actors enter
  • Tactical sophistication increases
  • Repression intensifies
  • Political rhetoric becomes more existential
  • Time between incidents decreases
  • Fatalities or injuries increase
  • Previously separate grievances converge
  • Institutions show declining control
  • Mobilization persists despite suppression
Condition Modifier
No acceleration 0
Limited acceleration +3
Clear acceleration +6
Rapid nonlinear acceleration +10

Nonlinear acceleration means several domains are worsening simultaneously rather than one category increasing gradually.

09

VIII. Flashpoint Trigger Modifier

Trigger Score (T) = Probability (P) × Resonance (R) × Mobilization Readiness (M)

Each component is scored from 1 to 5, producing a maximum raw trigger score of 125.

Trigger Score Modifier
1-25 0
26-50 +2
51-75 +5
76-100 +8
101-125 +12

A highly resonant trigger should receive a high modifier only when the surrounding political environment can transmit it into collective action.

10

IX. Containment and Resilience Modifier

Escalation risk must be balanced against the capacity of political and social systems to absorb shocks.

  • Credible electoral institutions
  • Independent courts
  • Trusted mediators
  • Professional and restrained policing
  • Opposition access to lawful political channels
  • Free and credible media
  • Responsive local government
  • Functioning legislative oversight
  • Internal government reform mechanisms
  • Labor dispute-resolution systems
  • Religious or traditional mediation networks
  • Trusted civil-society organizations
  • Government willingness to negotiate
  • Public confidence that peaceful action can produce change
  • Security-force discipline
  • Elite consensus against violence
Score Resilience Condition Modifier
0 Absent or collapsing 0
1 Very weak -2
2 Weak -4
3 Moderate -7
4 Strong -10
5 Highly resilient -15

Resilience should reduce risk only when the institutions are demonstrably capable of functioning during political stress. Formal institutions that exist only on paper should not receive full credit.

11

X. Final PU-FEAM Score

Final Score = Base Score + Trend Adjustment + Acceleration Modifier + Trigger Modifier - Resilience Modifier

The final score is capped between 0 and 100.

Score Warning Level Analytic Meaning
0-19 Level 1: Stable No significant escalation pathway is active.
20-34 Level 2: Watch Early pressure indicators require monitoring.
35-49 Level 3: Concern Multiple reinforcing escalation indicators are present.
50-64 Level 4: Flashpoint Risk A triggering event could produce substantial unrest.
65-79 Level 5: Escalation Probable Major unrest or political violence is plausible or developing.
80-100 Level 6: Crisis Transition Sustained violence, institutional fracture, or systemic instability is imminent or underway.
12

XI. Mandatory Escalation Overrides

Automatic Level 4 Minimum

  • Security forces use live ammunition against demonstrators
  • A major opposition leader is arrested immediately before an election
  • A nationwide internet shutdown coincides with mass arrests
  • Coordinated protests emerge in multiple major cities
  • Government declares a broad emergency targeting political activity
  • Protester deaths become a central mobilizing narrative

Automatic Level 5 Minimum

  • Security-force defections
  • Organized attacks on police or military facilities
  • Armed opposition cells emerge from protest networks
  • Multiple fatalities occur across separate demonstrations
  • Opposition establishes parallel governing structures
  • Large-scale communal or identity-based mobilization accompanies political unrest
  • Government deploys military forces with expanded lethal authorities

Automatic Level 6

  • Fragmentation of the armed forces
  • Competing claims to national executive authority
  • Coup, attempted coup, or military mutiny
  • Sustained nationwide violent unrest
  • Collapse of central government command
  • Coordinated armed seizure of government facilities
  • Widespread violence against civilian populations

Overrides require source validation and contextual review. A single unverified social-media report should never activate an override. The United Nations atrocity-risk framework also demonstrates the value of structured risk factors and observable indicators when assessing pathways from instability to organized violence [5].

XII. Event Coding Template

Field Description
Event ID Unique identifier
Date and time When the event occurred
Location Country, region, city, and coordinates when available
Event type Protest, riot, strike, arrest, speech, clash, killing, defection, negotiation, or related category
Actors Government, police, military, opposition, civil society, identity group, or external actor
Trigger Immediate precipitating issue
Grievance Underlying political or socioeconomic issue
Participation Estimated size and composition
Tactics Peaceful, disruptive, destructive, violent, or armed
State response None, negotiation, arrest, dispersal, or lethal force
Casualties Deaths and injuries
Geographic significance Local, regional, or national
Narrative significance Low, moderate, high, or iconic
Verification Number and quality of independent sources
Domain affected Relevant PU-FEAM indicator domain
Severity 0-5
Novelty Routine, unusual, or unprecedented
Direction Stabilizing, neutral, or escalatory
Related events Prior and subsequent linked incidents
Analyst note Context, uncertainty, and alternative explanations

ACLED’s event structure, which records event type, actors, location, date, and political context, provides a useful starting point for the register [1].

XIII. Pattern-of-Behavior Analysis

Pattern Analytic Question
Repetition The same behavior occurs repeatedly.
Expansion The behavior spreads geographically or across populations.
Intensification Severity increases.
Adaptation Actors learn and change tactics.
Coordination Formerly separate actors act together.
Institutionalization Temporary activities become enduring structures.
Retaliation Actions are increasingly driven by reciprocal punishment.
Normalization Exceptional behavior becomes routine.
Threshold Crossing An actor uses a tactic it previously avoided.
Contagion Tactics or narratives transfer between regions or countries.
15

XIV. Interaction Matrix

Interaction Escalation Significance
High grievance + low mobilization Latent unrest
High grievance + high mobilization Mass-unrest potential
High mobilization + high repression Confrontation and radicalization risk
High polarization + dehumanizing rhetoric Reduced restraint
Protester tactical escalation + state lethal force Violence feedback loop
Elite fragmentation + security-force division Regime-instability risk
Trigger proximity + high mobilization readiness Immediate flashpoint risk
Information restrictions + casualty rumors Misperception and panic risk
Closure of peaceful channels + organized opposition Underground or violent adaptation risk
Economic shock + contested election Grievance convergence

The matrix functions as a qualitative override that identifies reinforcing mechanisms hidden by the aggregate score.

16

XV. Source and Evidence Requirements

Each scored indicator should be supported by a source register containing source identity, publication date, event date, proximity to the event, independence, evidence type, reliability, corroboration status, known interests, information gaps, and confidence.

Recommended sourcing layers include:

  • Local reporting and direct observation
  • National media
  • International media and wire services
  • Government and opposition statements
  • Human-rights and civil-society reporting
  • Event datasets
  • Polling and survey data
  • Economic and institutional data
  • Verified imagery and geolocation
  • Social-media content used as an indicator rather than automatically treated as established fact

Strategic developments that do not involve immediate violence should still be recorded when they alter actor capabilities, political relationships, or future escalation potential [2].

17

XVI. Analytic Confidence

The PU-FEAM score and the analyst’s confidence must be reported separately. Confidence should be assessed across source reliability, source diversity, geographic coverage, temporal coverage, actor visibility, event verification, measurement consistency, and testing of alternative explanations.

Average Confidence
1.0-1.9 Low
2.0-2.9 Low to moderate
3.0-3.9 Moderate
4.0-4.5 Moderate to high
4.6-5.0 High

Example judgment: PU-FEAM Score 67, Level 5, Escalation Probable. Confidence: Moderate. Reporting demonstrates clear increases in repression, geographic protest diffusion, and opposition coordination, but security-force cohesion and rural sentiment remain poorly documented.

18

XVII. QAP Integration

1. Problem Statement

Define the political system, geographic scope, principal actors, observation period, and decision requirement.

2. Key Intelligence Questions

  • What mechanisms are most likely to convert existing political grievances into sustained civil unrest?
  • Are opposition groups developing the organization, resources, and public legitimacy required for sustained mobilization?
  • Is government suppression deterring mobilization or increasing opposition solidarity and radicalization?
  • What changes in political discourse indicate declining willingness to resolve disputes peacefully?
  • Are security forces likely to remain cohesive under sustained public pressure?
  • Which foreseeable events could serve as politically resonant flashpoint triggers?
  • What institutional or social mechanisms remain capable of containing escalation?
  • What indicators would demonstrate transition from protest activity to sustained political violence?
  • Which alternative explanations could account for apparent increases or declines in unrest?
  • What is the most likely trajectory over the next 30, 90, and 180 days?

3. Key Assumptions Check

  • Social-media activity reflects wider public sentiment
  • Declining protest totals indicate declining grievance
  • Opposition groups act as a unified bloc
  • Security forces will follow national leadership
  • Economic grievances will translate into political mobilization
  • Government concessions will reduce unrest
  • Violent incidents are attributable to declared actors

4. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses

  • H1: Unrest will remain localized and manageable.
  • H2: Demonstrations will expand but remain predominantly nonviolent.
  • H3: Repression will generate a cycle of radicalization and political violence.
  • H4: Elite or security-force fragmentation will transform civil unrest into a regime crisis.
  • H5: Government concessions or mediation will reduce escalation.
  • H6: Apparent instability is temporary and driven by a discrete event rather than systemic deterioration.

5. Indicators and Warnings

Translate PU-FEAM domains into observable indicators with predetermined thresholds.

6. Scenario Development

  • Most likely scenario
  • Escalatory scenario
  • De-escalatory scenario
  • High-impact, lower-probability scenario

7. Key Judgments

State the assessed trajectory, probability, timeframe, mechanism, and confidence.

19

XVIII. Recommended Analytic Output

BLUF

Provide a two- to four-sentence judgment stating the current warning level, direction of movement, principal escalation mechanism, most important trigger, and confidence.

Dashboard

Measure Illustrative Result
Base score 58
Trend Rapidly deteriorating
Acceleration Clear
Trigger modifier +8
Resilience adjustment -7
Final score 65
Warning level Level 5
Confidence Moderate

Supporting Components

  • Key Drivers: the three to five domains contributing most strongly to the score
  • Restraining Factors: institutions, behaviors, or conditions preventing further escalation
  • Flashpoint Calendar: foreseeable triggers during the next 30, 90, and 180 days
  • Indicator Watchlist: developments that would raise or lower the warning level
  • Geographic Hotspots: locations where grievance, mobilization, repression, and trigger conditions overlap
  • Actor Matrix: intent, capability, cohesion, legitimacy, escalation tolerance, vulnerability, and likely response to triggers
20

XIX. Operational Principle

The central judgment should not be limited to asking how much unrest is occurring. It should determine whether the political system is moving toward a condition in which actors increasingly believe that escalation is necessary, legitimate, effective, or unavoidable.

That question captures the transition from discontent to mobilization, from mobilization to confrontation, and from confrontation to a political flashpoint.
